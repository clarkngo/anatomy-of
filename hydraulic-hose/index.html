Anatomy of a hydraulic hose

It looks like a rubber tube. The rubber only seals; two layers of steel wire do the holding.

The parts

Point at a part to find it in the figure; click it to take the hose apart down to it.

  1. 01

    Tube

    The only layer that touches the oil: a seamless tube of synthetic rubber (usually nitrile) that keeps the fluid in. It seals, but it would burst at a small fraction of the working pressure on its own.

  2. 02

    Braids

    Two braids of high-tensile steel wire carry the load. They're laid at about 54.7°, the "neutral angle": there, the pull along the hose and the pull around it balance, so under pressure the hose barely grows longer or shorter. A second braid is what lifts a ½ in hose from about 16 MPa (one braid, R1) to 27.5 MPa (R2).

  3. 03

    Middle layer

    A thin layer of rubber between the braids bonds them together and stops the two wire layers rubbing through each other as the hose flexes and pulses.

  4. 04

    Cover

    Tough synthetic rubber that keeps water and grit off the wire, which would otherwise rust and fray. It carries the layline: the standard, the size, the working pressure and the date, so a fitter can tell exactly what the hose is.

  5. 05

    Fitting

    A barbed nipple goes inside the hose and a steel ferrule goes over it. A crimping press squeezes the ferrule down, pinching the hose between them for good. The swivel nut seals on a 37° cone (JIC), so it can be done up without twisting the hose.

  6. 06

    Test bench

    Each stroke of the hand pump pushes a little more oil into a closed hose, and the gauge climbs. The rated working pressure is 27.5 MPa (4,000 psi); the hose must hold at least four times that, 110 MPa, before it bursts. It shouldn't be bent tighter than 178 mm in radius.
